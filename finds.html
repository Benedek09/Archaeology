Copper Fragments

These pieces are a collection of ornately decorated copper fragments of various size, which likely originate from the Victorian period. In this era, copper had many uses, such as cookware due to its antimicrobial properties. Copper's reddish-brown hue also made it useful for decorative items, which is what these pieces were most probably a part of.

Brooch

This is a beautiful brooch or badge, that features acorns in the design, and looks as if it is iron - probably pressed - but is definitely some kind of worn metal. This pin was likely worn on clothes and likely dates to the 1800s or 1900s, as brooches were a popular item of self adornment then.

Bullet Casings

These are spent bullet casings, most of which are from .303 bullets. The .303 British is a 7.7mm calibre bullet that was the standard for British and Commonwealth rifles from 1889 to the 1950s. These were likely fired by soldiers of several units stationed in Woodford Halse in the build up to the D-Day Invasion. Both Woodford Halse and Hinton hosted three different British units who were waiting for D-Day.

Cartwheel Penny

This coin, minted in 1797, features King George III. The name 'cartwheel penny' is because of the coin's large size and resemblance to a cartwheel. It was created alongside the cartwheel twopence. Designed by German engraver Kuchler, they were minted by Matthew Boulton in his private Soho Mint in Birmingham. It is believed 40,000,000 of the 1797 penny were minted, so it is quite common.

Horseshoe Badge

Here we have another badge, this one taking the shape of a horseshoe, which may contain some lettering on the top portion of the curve. It also appears metallic, probably pressed iron, and its purpose is likely decorative. It originates from the late 19th or early 20th century.

Halfpenny

This is a very worn coin, but it is possible to make out that it is a halfpenny, featuring the Britannia. Possibly, the coin is from the reign of George II or George IV, most likely the former as the date under the Britannia appears to start '17' which would rule out George IV who reigned 1820 to 1830.

Barrel Key

This is a barrel key, a type of key featuring a distinctive barrel-like hole, they are used for purposes where it is not necessary to lock something securely but it is needed for something to be turned with a key. The design of this specific key suggests it might have been used to wind something, like a grandfather clock for example.

Military Button

This is a button that is likely from military uniform from the Gerogian Period, featuring a Tudor Crown (or King's Crown) that is commonly associated with George III.

Musket Ball

This is a damaged musket ball. It is of a traditional spherical shape, used in smoothbore muskets from their emergence in the early 16th century all the way to the mid-19th century when muskets became obsolete. However, later versions of the musket often used various other ammunition such as Minié balls that were of a conical shape - which is superior for aerodynamics - so this ball is likely from an older model.

George V Penny

Minted in 1918, this penny is in quite good condition. Looking at the Britannia on the front, it appears there is no initial underneath the shield, indicating this was a Royal Mint penny. Because this coin was minted in the war, some examples of it were not from the Royal Mint, bearing a 'KN' under the shield for Kings Norton Mint or more rarely 'H' for Heaton Mint.

WWI Peace Medal

This unintentionally curved medallion is a WWI peace medal, a type of commemorative medal that was distributed to mark the end of WWI. They come in many shapes and forms as many people made them. This particular medal is a 33mm piece from 1919, featuring a warrior presenting a sword to an angel on one side and the inscription 'To commemorate the victorious conclusion of the Great War' on the other.

Scallop Palm Guard

This scallop shaped 'palm guard' is quite unusual, many similar objects are found across the country, often being made of crudely cast lead. Some theorise 'palm guards' are simply puddles of waste lead, as specific uses for them are hard to find. However this specific piece appears to be moulded to fit the fingers of an individual. A potential use for such as item would be pushing steel needles through materials such as leather. It is the first scallop palm guard found in Northamptonshire.

Heart Pendant

This object is a heart-shaped, metal pendant - likely worn around the neck - and it seems to be hand made, due to the imperfections, potentially carved from a soft metal. There appears to be some kind of design or inscription in the centre of the heart and also on the back, but it is hard to make out what it could be.

Horse Buckle

This object is a brass horse buckle, that would have been used in the reigns of a horse, most likely in the 1800s. Brass was used in this period due to its resistance to corrosion and its durability - important factors for the outdoors.

Pendant Brooch

This is a pressed, pendant style brooch that likely held a locket. The metalwork suggests fairly modern origins, likely 19th or 20th century. The glass or enamel that formed the locket itself is unfortunately no longer in situ.
